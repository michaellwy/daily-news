Read this first: method, scope, and integrity caveats

This database includes only disputes that could be corroborated across real, named sources. Nothing here is reconstructed from memory. Where a field could not be verified, it is marked UNVERIFIED with a pointer to where it can be confirmed.

2. Sampling bias. This is a press-driven sample. Academic data (arXiv 2604.15674) finds Sports is the single largest dispute category (about 31.5 percent), yet this hand-built list captures only two sports cases, because the press over-covers politics, geopolitics, and novelty markets. Treat this as a high-significance sample, not a representative census.

Coverage: fourteen individually sourced disputes plus a verified structural and governance layer. A batching plan to extend the dataset is at the end.

How resolution and disputes work

Polymarket outsources resolution to UMA's Optimistic Oracle. A whitelisted proposer posts an outcome with a bond (reported at about 750 USDC.e). There is a short challenge window (about 2 hours). A disputer must match the bond. A disputed assertion escalates to UMA's DVM, where UMA token stakers vote through a commit-and-reveal cycle, typically resolving in roughly 48 to 96 hours. If no proposal reaches the consensus threshold, the vote can "roll" and voters get another window to discuss and revote. Voters in the majority earn rewards; dissenters lose a small fraction of staked UMA. This token-weighted vote is the mechanism at the center of most disputes below.

Mechanics sources: docs.uma.xyz/faqs; help.polymarket.com/en/articles/13364551-how-are-markets-disputed; startpolymarket.com/learn/how-markets-resolve.

Evidence that the problem is the rulebook and the resolver design, not just individual market wording.

  • In most disputed markets, more than 50% of voting power sits in the ten largest wallets.
  • At least 60% of active UMA voters over the prior year could be linked to live Polymarket accounts.
  • In nearly one in five disputes, at least one voter had a financial stake (other coverage: "more than 300 disputes").
  • Polymarket told the WSJ only 0.2% of contracts trigger UMA votes.
  • An anonymous voter ("Scout"): "You can either have traders with a conflict of interest, or morons with no conflict of interest." (Confirm verbatim.)

C. UMIP-189 / "MOOV2" governance reform Aug 2025

UMIP-189, "Approve new ManagedOptimisticOracleV2 Deployment," reportedly passed ~Aug 6, 2025. Transitions Polymarket from Optimistic Oracle V2 to Managed Optimistic Oracle V2; creates a whitelist (~37 vetted addresses) as the only parties who can directly submit proposals. Disputing remains open to anyone. Eligibility tied to a track record (sources conflict: "20+ proposals at 95%" vs "minimum 5 proposals, 95% over a rolling 6-month window"). Whitelist reportedly grew from 37 to ~177 by November 2025. Motivation: improve proposal quality after the March 2025 Ukraine attack.

Most frequent primary cause: ambiguous wording and undefined key terms (6 of 14). Markets turned on a word the rules never pinned down: "suit," "declassify," "perform," "permanent," "banned," and "Hezbollah" as an entity. Ordinary words the writers assumed were self-evident.

Most consequential and pervasive cause: oracle manipulation and whale voting. Primary in 3 cases but contributing in at least 5 more, so token-weighted voting touches 8 of 13 disputes (about 60 percent). Most consequential by dollars and reputation: Ukraine mineral (premature Yes), Fort Knox (No, manipulation alleged), UFO (Yes, no documents), Strategy (98.6% of voting power). The WSJ and Bloomberg investigations confirm this is structural.

The interaction is the core finding. Ambiguous wording creates a disputable market; whale voting decides it once it exists. Airtight wording rarely reaches the oracle (only 0.2% of contracts trigger a vote). So the rules-design lesson is twofold: tighten wording to keep markets out of the oracle, and fix the oracle for the residual that still gets there.

A third, separate failure mode deserves its own line: the optimistic-oracle challenge-window gap (Astros-Dodgers, entry 14). Nothing was ambiguous and no whale was needed; a wrong answer was posted and nobody disputed it inside the ~2-hour window, so it finalized on-chain and Polymarket refunded users off-protocol. This is the design's quiet baseline risk: it assumes someone is always watching, and the academic data (sports ~31.5% of disputes, mostly resolved quietly at the oracle layer) suggests low-salience markets are exactly where unchallenged errors hide. The same lapsed-window mechanic is what lets a whale's proposal stand in the high-profile cases, so fixing the watcher problem (more proposers, longer or staked challenge windows, automated cross-checks) addresses both.

Written summary of the patterns

  1. The failure is definitional, not factual. The underlying facts were rarely disputed (Zelenskyy wore a blazer; Cardi B danced without singing; Strategy sold 32 BTC and disclosed a day late; the ceasefire was with Lebanon's government). What was disputed was whether those agreed facts satisfied an underspecified word.
  2. "Consensus of credible reporting" is the single most dangerous phrase in the dataset. It appears in the Zelenskyy, UFO, Trump-Xi, and (as fallback) Venezuela markets, converting a factual question into a subjective vote about whether enough outlets agreed.
  3. Source hierarchies and entity definitions that point two ways are a structural trap. Venezuela (official vs credible reporting) and Israel-Hezbollah (Hezbollah vs the Lebanese government) both guaranteed conflict once reality split the difference.
  4. Timing and disclosure are under-specified almost everywhere. Strategy shows "by date X" is ambiguous between occurrence and disclosure; Thailand shows "Too Early" is a timing judgment whales can game.
  5. The resolver design is now the story. Through 2024 the disputes read as wording fights; by 2025–2026 the dominant narrative is oracle capture. Polymarket has had to publicly override its own oracle (Barron Trump, Trump-Xi), undercutting the "decentralized resolution" claim. UMIP-189 and Chainlink are admissions the original design did not hold.
  6. Cross-platform divergence is your strongest evidence. Cardi B resolved Yes on Polymarket and was refunded on Kalshi on identical footage. Rule-writing, not reality, did the work.
  7. The "morally right, procedurally wrong" pattern recurs. Venezuela, Thailand, and Trump-Xi all reached the outcome most observers consider correct, but by overriding stated rules or surviving whale distortion. A resolver that reaches the right answer the wrong way is still broken, because the next case may reach the wrong answer by the same mechanism.
